Class 2 · Specify, iterate, keep what works

By the end of this session QuoteDesk keeps quote requests for every rep behind a login, has a history you can roll back, and your team’s assistant knows how Cerro Azul writes a quote and where the price list lives.

Session 2 · 4 hours Build goal: database, login, rollback, one skill, one connector Milestone: M2 due before session 3

The session is 4 hours. The framework block is a recap of this page, which you read before class, so that most of the time goes to building. The build lab includes a 10-minute break.

How to use this page
  • Read the topics in order. Each one opens with a guiding question and uses the Cerro Azul case.
  • Every topic has at least one simulator. Predict what will happen before you touch it, then check.
  • The “In the business” note says why the idea matters to a manager, not to a programmer.
  • The practice questions explain why each wrong option is wrong. Retry as often as you like.
  • The build lab is what your team does in class. Its prompts are meant to be copied and adapted, and the checklist is saved in your browser.
  • Data rule: until your team has completed the Class 4 lab, use only invented or de-identified messages. No real names, phones, addresses or tax numbers in the app, in the test set or in any prompt.

What you will be able to do

  • Write a user story with acceptance criteria that an assistant can build without guessing.
  • Work in small requests, test by using the app, save checkpoints, and roll back a bad change without reading code.
  • Move QuoteDesk from a page that forgets everything to a managed database with a login for each rep.
  • Specify four UX patterns for output that is sometimes wrong.
  • Tell apart the three things people call “the AI”: the assistant you build with, the assistant a rep works with, and the model inside the app.
  • Write a project instructions file, and package “how we write a quote here” as a skill the assistant loads on its own.
  • Connect your team’s assistant to one company system through a connector, read-only, and say in one sentence what that door allows.
  • Deliver milestone M2: the deployed prototype v1, with one skill and one connector.

Topic 1 · Prompting as specification

Guiding question: if the assistant builds exactly what you asked, why does it so often build the wrong thing?

In session 1 your team wrote a brief and had a working page in under an hour. The second hour usually feels different: the assistant adds a button you never asked for, the list sorts the wrong way, “save” works on your laptop and not on your phone. None of that is the assistant being careless. It is the assistant filling the gaps you left with the most common answer from millions of other apps. A quote tool for a food distributor in Chile is not the most common app.

The fix is a habit, not a tool. Before you ask for a feature, write it as a user story with acceptance criteria. A user story is one sentence: as a sales rep, I want to paste a customer message and save it as a quote request, so that I stop retyping it into the ERP. Acceptance criteria are the three to six checks that tell you, and the assistant, when the story is done. With fewer than three you leave the decisions to the assistant. With more than six you have probably glued two stories together.

Part Weak Strong
User “users” “a Cerro Azul sales rep on a phone, between customer visits”
Action “handle quotes” “paste a WhatsApp message and save it as a quote request for one named customer”
Done when “it works” “the request appears at the top of the list with date, rep and customer; empty messages are refused”

Managers know this format from product teams. What is new is that the assistant reads it literally. Each criterion you write is one decision it does not have to guess. Each one you leave out is a surprise waiting in the next hour.

In the business

Paula retyping a quote costs Cerro Azul about 12 minutes. A manager who specifies badly costs more: every vague request is a build, a test, a complaint and a rebuild. The assistant is cheap per attempt, but your team’s two hours in the lab are not. Acceptance criteria are the cheapest insurance you will buy in this course.

Practice · Topic 1

Which of these is an acceptance criterion, as opposed to a wish?

A criterion is a check that is either true or false when you use the app. "Cannot be saved when empty" is one. "Fast and easy" is a wish, and the assistant will decide for you what it means.

The assistant added a "priority" dropdown to the quote request that nobody asked for. What most likely happened?

Every unspecified field is a guess. Listing the fields (customer, message, date, rep, status) in the criteria removes the guess, and the dropdown.

Topic 2 · Small steps, checkpoints and undo

Guiding question: how do you make it safe, and fast, to break the app?

Every first-time builder repeats one pattern. Confidence is high, so the team pastes a single request: login, customer list, quote form, PDF export, email notification, dashboard. The assistant produces something that mostly runs. Then the login breaks the list, the PDF breaks the form, and nobody knows which of the six changes caused which of the four problems.

The alternative is boring and it works: one story per request. Ask for the customer picker. Use the app as Paula would, on a phone. If it works, save a checkpoint. If it does not, you know what broke, because only one thing changed.

The rule has a second half: test by using, not by reading. You are not going to read the code and you do not need to. Do the rep’s task end to end, including the cases that should fail. When something looks wrong, ask: what did you change and why? The answer is written in plain language, and it is often where you find the gap in your criteria.

Small steps are only cheap if going back is cheap. Software teams solve that with version control (the usual tool is Git, with the history kept online on GitHub). You will use it through the assistant, so you need three ideas, not the commands:

  • A checkpoint (a commit) is a named snapshot of the whole app at a moment it worked. “Customer picker working” is a good name. Save one after every story that passes your test, and at least every 20 minutes.
  • A branch is an experiment: a copy where you try something risky without touching the version that works. If it is good you merge it. If not, you discard it and nothing happened.
  • Rollback returns the app to any earlier checkpoint, in seconds. The worst case of a bad request becomes “go back twenty minutes”.

Rollback has a limit worth knowing before you rely on it. It restores the code. It does not restore what is in the database, emails that were already sent, or what customers already saw. A change that deletes or rewrites data needs its own care: ask the assistant what the change does to existing rows before you approve it.

You ask for all of it in plain language: save a checkpoint called “login working”, start a branch to try a new layout, go back to the checkpoint before the PDF export.

In the business

This is how good operations teams already work: change one thing on the line, measure, keep or revert. The history is also your audit trail. When Diego asks why the discount field disappeared last Tuesday, the timeline answers in one click, and when you hand the prototype to engineering in session 4, the record of what was tried and why is worth more to them than the code.

Practice · Topic 2

After one request for three features, the quote list stopped loading. You described the bug precisely once and the fix did not work. What is the fastest way forward?

One precise description is worth trying: a bug is a conversation. When that fails on a tangle of changes, going back to the last state that worked and adding the changes one by one isolates the cause in minutes. This is why checkpoints and small steps belong together.

When should your team save a checkpoint?

A checkpoint is a state you would be happy to return to. Name it after what works, so rolling back is a choice between meaningful states and not between timestamps.

Diego wants to try a radical redesign of the quote list while Paula's login story is half built. What should he do?

A branch is exactly this: an experiment that cannot break the working app. Merge it when it passes the test, or discard it at no cost.

Topic 3 · Where the data lives

Guiding question: why did the quote requests disappear when Paula refreshed the page?

QuoteDesk v0 kept its quote requests in the page: they existed only in the browser that created them, as “today’s list on that device”. Refresh, and they are gone. Open the app on Diego’s phone, and the list is empty. That is normal for a first hour and useless for a second rep.

Data can live in three places. In the page: instant, free, gone on refresh. In a file on the server: survives refresh, but two reps saving at once can overwrite each other and nothing says who is who. In a managed database: a service that stores rows, serves many users at once, keeps backups, and comes with authentication (the login: who are you) built in. For anything two people will use, the database is the answer, and the assistant sets it up in a few requests.

The login matters beyond security. It ties each quote request to the rep who created it, and it lets the app have two roles from today: rep and manager, with Carla’s account as the manager. In v1 the quote requests are a shared queue that every signed-in rep sees, so that anyone can pick up a waiting customer. Priced quotes, with their discounts, will be private to the rep who owns the customer and to Carla. That second rule is not built by a login; it is the subject of session 4.

In the business

“Where does the data live?” is the first question legal, IT and finance will ask, long before they ask what the app does. “In a managed database, in a named region, with a login per rep” is the answer that lets a prototype be piloted with real customers. “On my laptop” is the answer that ends the meeting.

Practice · Topic 3

Paula and Diego save quote requests at the same moment. Which storage choice handles this safely?

A managed database is built for many people writing at once and records who created each row. The other two break as soon as there is a second person.

QuoteDesk v1 has a login. Marcos signs in. What has the app established?

Authentication answers "who are you". Authorization answers "what may you do". QuoteDesk v1 needs the first; assuming it gives you the second is the mistake session 4 opens with.

Topic 4 · UX for output that is sometimes wrong

Guiding question: Paula will trust QuoteDesk once. What makes her trust it the second time?

Next session QuoteDesk will read a pasted message and propose the items: 20 cajas of aceite 900 cc, 5 sacos of arroz, “lo de siempre de lácteos”. The last one is a guess. If the app shows that guess with the same confidence as the first two, Paula learns after one wrong delivery that nothing on the screen can be trusted. Then she checks every line, and the app saves nobody any time.

Four patterns fix this. They are decisions you write into the story, not into the code:

  • Show confidence. Lines the model is unsure about look different and say why.
  • Stream the result. Items appear as they are produced, so a three-second wait looks like progress and not like a frozen screen.
  • Honest errors. When there is nothing to extract, the app says “no items found in this message” and offers the manual form, not an empty table.
  • Let the user correct. Every proposed line can be edited in place and the correction is saved. The app is never the last word.

The model will be wrong some of the time whatever you do. The experience decides whether being wrong costs a glance or costs a customer.

In the business

Trust can be measured: how often reps edit a proposed line, how often they give up and retype, how many wrong deliveries trace back to a quote. The correction button gives you that data for free, and in session 3 those corrections become your test set.

Practice · Topic 4

The model is unsure what "lo de siempre de lácteos" means for Doña Elsa. What should the screen do?

Visible uncertainty plus an easy correction is the pattern. Paula spends two seconds on the one line that needs her and trusts the rest.

A customer writes only "hola, me llamas?". What should QuoteDesk show?

An honest error says what happened and what the user can do next. It keeps Paula inside the tool.

Topic 5 · Teach the assistant how you work: project instructions and skills

Guiding question: you have explained Cerro Azul's quote format to the assistant four times this week. Where should that explanation live so that you never type it again?

Three things people call “the AI”

Before teaching “the assistant” anything, be exact about which one. From this session on, three different things are in play, and most confusion in AI projects comes from blurring them.

What it is Who uses it What it can reach
The building assistant The agentic coding assistant your team builds QuoteDesk with Your team, while building The repository, plus the project instructions, skills and connectors you give it
A rep’s assistant The assistant Paula or Carla uses directly for daily work: drafting a quote, summarizing calls, the morning digest A rep or a manager, every day The same skills and connectors, with that person’s own access
The model inside QuoteDesk The model that the deployed app calls through an API Nobody directly; the app calls it Only what the app sends it, and only the tools the app defines. No skills, no connectors

The consequence matters for your milestone. Everything in this topic and the next extends the first two: the skill and the connector you deliver in M2 belong to your team’s assistants, not to the deployed app. QuoteDesk itself cannot open a skill or pass through a connector. In session 3 it gets its prices from its own products table, imported from the price sheet.

Project instructions and skills

The assistant starts every session knowing nothing about Cerro Azul. It does not know that a quote request is not a quote, that a caja of aceite holds 12 bottles, or that only Carla approves discounts. So far you have repeated these things in every prompt. There are two better places to put them, and they do different jobs.

Project instructions are a plain-text file in the project that the assistant reads at the start of every session. Different tools give it different names (for example CLAUDE.md or AGENTS.md); the idea is the same. It holds what is always true: what the product is, your vocabulary, the rules (“one story per change”, “all money in CLP without decimals”, “ask before touching the login”) and how you want changes reported. Because it is always loaded, every line is paid for in every session. Keep it under a page.

Skills hold procedures that are needed only sometimes. A skill is a folder with a file called SKILL.md: a name, a one-line description that says when to use it, and the steps. It can carry other files too: a template, examples, even small scripts. The assistant sees only the names and descriptions of your skills until a request matches one, and then it opens that folder and follows it. This is called progressive disclosure, and it is why twenty installed skills cost almost nothing until one is used. The format was published as an open standard in December 2025 and dozens of assistants and coding tools support it, so a skill written for one tool usually works in another.

Loaded Good for At Cerro Azul
A prompt Once, now A one-off request “Summarize yesterday’s calls”
Project instructions Every session, always Rules and vocabulary that never change “Money in CLP, no decimals. Never change the login without asking.”
A skill Only when a request matches its description A procedure with steps, a template and examples “How we write a quote”, “the Monday sales note”

One detail decides whether a skill works: the description is the trigger. It is the only part the assistant reads before choosing. “Formats quotes” gives it nothing to match. “Drafts or checks a Cerro Azul quote in the house format. Use whenever the user asks for a quote, cotización or price offer for a customer” tells it what the skill does and when to open it. Try both below.

One caution for later. A skill from the internet is a stranger’s instructions, and sometimes a stranger’s code, running with your access. Read it yourself before you install it; the assistant may help you read it, but its verdict is not the control. Session 4 treats this as what it is: a supply chain.

In the business

A skill is how know-how stops living in Paula’s head. When Carla changes the discount rule, she edits one file and every assistant that has the skill installed follows the new rule from the next request. That is process documentation that executes itself, and it deserves what any process document gets: an owner, a date, and a review when the process changes.

Practice · Topic 5

"All money in CLP without decimals. Never change the login without asking first." Where do these two rules belong?

What is always true goes in project instructions, which are always loaded. What is needed sometimes, and has steps, goes in a skill.

Your team installed the quote-format skill. Paula asks her assistant for a quote for Doña Elsa and gets a generic letter with an invented 5% discount. What is the most likely cause?

The description is the trigger. If the skill is right and the output ignores it, fix the description first: say what the skill does and when to use it, in the words a rep would use.

Diego finds a "sales-email-writer" skill online with thousands of downloads and wants to install it for the team. What comes first?

A skill is instructions, and sometimes code, that will act with your permissions. It is short enough to read in five minutes. Read it yourself, and prefer writing your own for anything that touches customers.

Topic 6 · Connect the assistant to your systems: connectors and MCP

Guiding question: the price list lives in Drive and the customers live in the ERP. How does your team's assistant reach them without you copying and pasting?

Until now the assistant has known only what you pasted into it. Ask it what aceite 900 cc costs for Minimarket Los Aromos and it has two honest options: say it does not know, or guess. The answer sits in two systems it cannot see.

A connector gives the assistant a door into one of those systems. Underneath, most connectors use the same open standard, the Model Context Protocol (MCP), introduced in late 2024 and since handed to a neutral foundation, so that a connector built once works with many assistants. Each system is presented by a small program called a server, which offers three things:

A server offers Which means At Cerro Azul
Tools Actions the assistant can ask for “look up a price”, “find a customer”, “send an email”
Resources Data it can read the price list, the delivery policy document
Prompts Ready-made requests “weekly price changes”

You rarely build one. For Drive, email, calendars, the common CRMs and databases they already exist, and in most assistants you switch them on in a settings screen. Signing in uses the “Sign in with…” window you already know (the standard is called OAuth), and it has a consequence worth saying aloud: the assistant gets at most the access of the person who signed in, narrowed by the permissions the connector asks for. If Paula cannot open the payroll folder, neither can her assistant. What Paula can open, her assistant can usually open too. One exception to watch: a connector set up with a shared service account gives everyone who uses it that account’s access, whoever they are.

That makes every connector a decision about access. Three rules cover most of it:

  1. Read before write. A connector that can only read can produce a wrong answer. One that can send or write can produce a wrong action.
  2. One system at a time. Add a door, use it for a week, then add the next. If five open at once you cannot tell which one caused the problem.
  3. What comes back is data, not instructions. A document or an email fetched through a connector may contain text that looks like an order. Session 4 shows what happens when the assistant obeys it.

Skills and connectors complete each other. The skill is how we write a quote. The connector is where the prices are. With both, Paula’s one-line request to her assistant produces a quote in the house format with real prices. Remember whose doors these are: they belong to the team’s assistants. The deployed QuoteDesk does not pass through them.

In the business

A manager should be able to say, in one sentence, which systems the assistant can read, which it can change, and who approved that. Start with the price list, read-only: the worst case is a wrong number on Paula’s screen, with the source one click away. Email and ERP orders are a different conversation, and they come with approval in session 3 and with permissions in session 4.

Practice · Topic 6

Paula's assistant now drafts quotes in the right house format, but the prices are invented. What is missing?

The skill is how; the connector is where. Invented prices mean the assistant has the procedure and no access to the data.

Which connector should the team switch on first for its assistants?

Read before write, one system at a time. Pick the door with the most value and the smallest worst case, and open the next one after a week of use.

Paula connects her assistant to Drive by signing in with her own account. What can the assistant read?

The assistant can reach at most what Paula can open, narrowed by the permissions the connector asks for. If that is too wide, share a single folder with a dedicated account, or limit the connector to that folder.

Build lab · QuoteDesk v1, one skill, one connector

What your team builds in the next 120 minutes

  • One experiment was tried on a branch and merged or discarded.
  • The honest empty state and the loading state are in the app.
  • The quote-format skill triggers in a new session without being named, and stays closed for an unrelated request.
  • One connector is on in the team's assistant, read-only, and its answer was checked against the source.
  • The app is deployed and opened on a phone, no key was pasted into a chat, and every build log is up to date.

Critique and reflection

Two teams show one user story end to end: paste a message, pick the customer, save, refresh, sign in as a second rep. The class then asks each presenting team for two things live: roll back one change, and make the quote-format skill trigger without naming it. The rubric criterion in focus this session is functionality: does the product work for its user end to end, including the cases that should fail?

Reflection prompt for your build log: what did the assistant guess that you should have specified, and which line would you now add to your acceptance criteria, your project instructions or your skill?

Summary

  • The assistant builds what you specify and guesses the rest. Three to six acceptance criteria remove the guesses.
  • One story per request. Test by using, not by reading. Ask “what did you change and why?”
  • A checkpoint is a state that worked, with a name. A branch is an experiment that cannot break the working app. Rollback makes small steps cheap, and it restores the code, not the data or the emails already sent.
  • Data for two or more people belongs in a managed database with a login. Authentication is “who are you”; authorization comes in session 4.
  • Four UX patterns for output that is sometimes wrong: visible confidence, streaming, honest errors, user correction.
  • Three things are called “the AI”: the building assistant, a rep’s assistant, and the model inside QuoteDesk. Skills and connectors extend the first two, never the deployed app.
  • Project instructions hold what is always true and are always loaded, so keep them short. A skill holds a procedure and is opened only when a request matches its description. The description is the trigger.
  • A connector is a door into one system, with at most the access of whoever signed in. Read before write, one system at a time, and treat what comes back as data.
