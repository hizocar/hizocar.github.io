Class 5 · Demo Day and strategy revisited

By the end of the session you will have presented QuoteDesk to a panel with a deck you generated and checked, graded it against the rubric, decided what in it is defensible, and written your reflection memo.

Session 5 · 4 hours Build goal: none. The product is frozen 24 hours before. Deliverables: Demo Day (25%) · reflection memo and participation (15%)

The session is 4 hours. The pitch clinic is a recap of topics 1 to 3 of this page, which you read before class; the strategy block discusses topics 4 and 5.

How to use this page
  • Read it before Demo Day, in order. Topics 1 to 3 prepare the pitch: the deck, the ten minutes, the rubric. Topics 4 and 5 are the strategy block we discuss after the pitches.
  • Every topic opens with a guiding question, explains the idea with Cerro Azul and QuoteDesk, gives you one simulator, an “In the business” note and a short practice set.
  • The deck builder, the pitch timer and the rubric self-scorer are tools for your rehearsal. Use them with your team the day before.
  • The reflection memo builder keeps your draft in this browser. Draft the five parts before class and finish them in the last block.
  • The Demo Day checklist is also saved in your browser. Check it off the night before.

What you will be able to do

  • Generate a deck with an AI assistant from your own sources, with one message per slide and every number traced to a file you can open.
  • Deliver a 10-minute live demonstration of a working product and its business case to a panel of decision makers, and answer their questions.
  • Grade a product against the course rubric and name the one change that would raise the grade most.
  • Explain which parts of what you built are defensible when anyone can build the same thing in a weekend, and which are not.
  • Order the steps that take a vibe-coded prototype to a production system, and say who owns each one.
  • Write a reflection memo that separates what the AI did from what you decided, and leave with a small AI work system of your own: skills, connectors and one automation.

Topic 1 · Build the deck with AI

Guiding question: The assistant can produce twelve slides in a minute. Which of them would you put your name under in front of the panel?

By now the raw material for the pitch exists: your build log, the results of the 24-message test set, the risk memo, screenshots of QuoteDesk and the cost model. An assistant with a presentation skill turns those files into a deck in about the time it takes to read this paragraph. A presentation skill is a skill like the one you wrote in Class 2, except that it knows how to produce slides in a given format. Most assistants ship with one built in, and your instructor shares one for the course. Without a skill, ask for an HTML deck, which any assistant can write. The output is an HTML deck you open in a browser or a PowerPoint file. The deck is no longer the work. The judgment is.

Storyline first. Before any slide exists, write six sentences, one for each block of the pitch in Topic 2. Sentences, not labels. “Problem” is a label. “Cerro Azul’s 14 reps retype about 120 quote requests a day, and a quote goes out 3 to 6 hours later” is a message the panel can agree or disagree with. Each sentence becomes the title of one slide, and a slide carries one message. If you cannot write the sentence, the slide has nothing to say.

Every number needs a source. The assistant does not leave gaps. Ask it for a roadmap slide without giving it the cost model and it will write “saves 70% of rep time”, because decks usually say something like that. That is the failure to watch for. The rule is simple: a number on a slide must be traceable to one of your files, or it comes off.

Block The message, as a sentence Where the number must come from
Problem 14 reps retype about 120 requests a day; a quote takes 3 to 6 hours The brief (M1) and the five timed requests from M3, in the build log
Live demo Paula quotes Doña Elsa’s request in under two minutes The product itself; a screenshot holds the slide
Evidence On 24 de-identified messages, the guardrail cut unnoticed wrong quotes from 6 to 1 Test-set results
Risks Three findings fixed, one still open Risk memo
Decision Keep vibe coding QuoteDesk; hand the ERP write-back to engineering Risk memo and the decision matrix from Class 4
Roadmap Three conditions for rollout and about USD 28 a month in model calls Cost model

The roadmap figure is the kind of number to check twice: about 2,600 requests a month on a mid-sized model at the illustrative prices from Class 3, under the USD 50 monthly limit you set in Class 4. Prices change, so the slide names its source and the date.

The demo stays live. The deck reserves one slide for it, with a screenshot that holds the screen while the product loads. A 90-second recording is the fallback if the deployed app fails, and you say out loud that it is a recording.

AI use is declared, here as everywhere in the course. The closing slide names the sources and says the slides were generated with an assistant and checked by the team. The prompts go in the build log.

The public page from Class 1 becomes the demo page. Update it with what is now true and give the panel its address on the closing slide. The same rule applies: no claim on it that your sources cannot support.

A side example. Imagine an HR team that built a tool for onboarding contractors and asks the assistant for “a deck about our project”. It returns 19 slides, including one on the future of work. The team deletes every slide whose title is not one of their six sentences and presents eight.

Three prompts, in this order. The first produces no slides at all, on purpose.

You, to the assistant · 1 of 3, the storyline
Read the attached build log, test-set results, risk memo and cost model for QuoteDesk. Do not make slides yet. Write the storyline for a 10-minute pitch to a panel of three managers as six full sentences, one for each block: problem, live demo, evidence, risks, decision, roadmap. Each sentence states one claim. If it contains a number, name the file and the line the number comes from. If a block has no evidence in the files, write MISSING instead of a sentence and tell me what I would need to measure.
You, to the assistant · 2 of 3, the slides
Use your presentation skill; if you have none, produce an HTML deck. Turn the six approved sentences below into a deck of eight slides: a title slide, one slide per sentence with the sentence as its title, and a closing slide that lists the sources, gives the address of our demo page, and says the deck was generated with an AI assistant and checked by the team. One message per slide, at most 25 words of body text, no stock images, no slide I did not ask for. Print the source under every number in small type. For the live demo slide use the screenshot quote-screen.png and the words "Live demo". Then list every number you used with its source, and list anything you wanted to add and did not.
You, to the assistant · 3 of 3, the one-page handout
From the same six sentences and the same sources, write a one-page handout the panel can keep: the problem in two lines, what QuoteDesk does in three, a small table with the test-set result before and after the guardrail, the three risk findings with their status, our decision, and the three conditions for rollout with an owner and a cost for each. No adjectives about the product. Same numbers as the deck, same sources. End with one line saying the deck and this page were drafted with an AI assistant and checked by the team.
In the business

For a long time the deck was the bottleneck: days of someone’s time. When a deck costs a minute, more decks appear, and the scarce thing becomes the reader’s trust in the numbers. A manager whose decks are believed asks “where does this number come from” of every generated slide, starting with their own. Ask it of the decks your teams send you too.

Practice · Topic 1

The generated deck has a slide that says "QuoteDesk reduces quoting time by 70%". Nothing in your build log supports that figure. What do you do?

The assistant filled a gap with a plausible figure. Either the number is traceable to a file of yours or it does not go in front of the panel. Five timed requests is a small sample, and it is real.

Which of these is a storyline sentence, not a label?

A sentence makes a claim that can be checked against a source. That is what lets one slide carry one message, and what lets you cut every slide that has no sentence.

The assistant returned 14 slides for a 10-minute slot that includes a 3-minute live demo. What is the best fix?

Generating slides is free; the panel's attention is not. The storyline is the filter: six sentences, six slides, plus title and sources. Anything else answers a question nobody has asked yet.

Topic 2 · The ten minutes that matter

Guiding question: You have ten minutes with three people who can fund QuoteDesk or stop it. What do they need to see, and in what order?

The panel on Demo Day is the instructor plus two invited managers or alumni. They have not read your milestones. They form a first opinion of QuoteDesk in the first minute or two and spend the rest of the time testing it. Your job is to give them the evidence in the order they need it, not in the order you built it.

The pitch has six blocks, the same six as your storyline.

Block Time What goes in it
Problem 1:00 Cerro Azul’s reps retype about 120 quote requests a day, a quote takes 3 to 6 hours, and the number that matters is quote time.
Live demo 3:00 One user story, end to end, with a de-identified message. Paula pastes it, QuoteDesk extracts the items and flags the line it is unsure about (“lo de siempre de lácteos”), Paula corrects it, the quote is saved. If a discount appears, Carla approves it. Nothing else.
Evidence 2:00 The test set: 24 messages, the result before and after the guardrail, and what the review workload looks like.
Risks 1:30 The three findings from your risk memo that a manager would want to know, and what you did about each.
Decision 1:00 The build / buy / vibe decision: keep vibe coding it, hand it to engineering to build properly, buy a product, or stop, with the reasoning.
Roadmap 1:30 What has to be true to put this in front of 14 reps, who does it, and roughly what it costs.

Two rules that are easy to break. First, the demo is live. Use the deployed app; if it fails, play the recording and say that it is a recording. Screenshots alone tell the panel you do not trust your product, and a failure you name becomes a risk finding, which is what the risks block is for. Second, the business case is not a slide of benefits. It is the metric from your brief (three reps use it for two weeks and quote time drops below one hour), the number you measured in M3 on at least five timed requests, and the gap between them.

A side example. Imagine a dental clinic team that built a tool for rescheduling appointments. Their first rehearsal spends six minutes on how retrieval works. Their second opens with the receptionist’s screen at 8:05 on a Monday, 40 unread rescheduling requests, and the tool proposing new slots for them in twelve seconds. The panel asks about retrieval themselves, which is where you want that question to come from.

In the business

Executives are used to pitches that end with “and so we need budget”. A working demo changes the conversation: the question is no longer whether the idea could work but what it would take to roll it out. Use that. Spend the roadmap block on the three conditions for rollout and let the panel do the arithmetic. A team that asks for a decision, not for enthusiasm, is more likely to get one.

Practice · Topic 2

Your deployed QuoteDesk stops responding five minutes before your slot. What do you do with the demo block?

The recording still shows the real product completing the story. A deploy that broke under pressure is exactly the kind of finding the panel wants to hear you name, and naming it earns more trust than hiding it.

Which opening line gives the panel the most in the first fifteen seconds?

User, pain, number, then the screen. The panel now knows what QuoteDesk is for, how to judge it, and that you are about to show it working.

Topic 3 · How the panel grades you

Guiding question: If you could improve one thing in QuoteDesk tonight, which one would raise your grade most?

Demo Day is 25% of the course grade. It is graded as a team, with an individual adjustment of up to ten points from the build log and the peer assessment. That adjustment applies to the Demo Day grade only. The class grades with the rubric too, and the panel’s grade is the one that counts. The rubric has four criteria, the same four used for every graded component of the course:

Criterion What the panel looks for Where it shows in the pitch
Problem and value Is the problem real, the user named, the metric measurable Problem block, roadmap
Functionality Does the product work for its user, end to end Demo
AI quality and safety Is the model capability useful, evaluated and guarded Evidence, risks
Communication Are the product and the decision explained clearly to this audience All of it, and the questions

Where a criterion does not apply to a component, it is left out. M1, a brief with no product yet, was graded on problem and value and on communication. The reflection memo is graded on AI quality and safety (what the AI did, what you decided, where it misled you) and on communication. The level descriptors for every component are on the rubric page.

Each criterion has four levels. Level 1 is “absent or not credible”. Level 4 is “a manager could act on this tomorrow”. Expect to arrive at level 3 on functionality, because you have been building for four sessions, and at level 2 on problem and value if the brief from session 1 was never revisited. That is the cheapest grade to recover: the metric you wrote in M1, measured in M3 on at least five timed requests, moves you a full level.

The simulator lets you score QuoteDesk as the panel would. It converts levels into an approximate grade: level 2 across the board is about 55, the pass mark, and level 3 is about 80. Look for the criterion that is lowest in your own case and is one evening of work away from the next level.

In the business

Large organizations decide on prototypes with a rubric too, usually an unwritten one. A steering committee asks the same four questions: is the problem worth it, does it work, can we trust it, do I understand it. Writing your own rubric before an internal demo, and scoring yourself honestly, is the cheapest way to hear the surprise questions before the committee asks them. Take the habit with you.

Practice · Topic 3

A team's QuoteDesk extracts items perfectly in the demo, but the team never did the five timed requests for M3. Which criterion suffers most?

Without the metric from the brief the panel cannot tell whether QuoteDesk solves the problem or just impresses. Problem and value stays at level 2 however good the extraction is.

How does the individual adjustment work?

The build log is the evidence of what each person did and decided. The peer assessment at the end of the session confirms it. Together they move the Demo Day grade up or down by at most ten points per person, and no other component.

Which single addition is most likely to move a team from level 2 to level 3 on "AI quality and safety"?

"Evaluated and guarded" is the definition of the criterion. A count before, a count after, and the cost in human review is the evidence that you know what the model gets wrong and what you did about it.

Demo Day · Run of show

What happens in the first 180 minutes of the session

Pitch clinic 30 min · then 8 teams, 10 minutes plus 5 of questions each · panel: the instructor and two invited managers or alumni · break after team 4

  1. Pitch clinic (30 min). The order is drawn. Every team opens its deployed address, its recording and its deck on the classroom screen once, then says its opening line aloud. The instructor recaps the six blocks and the rubric. Nobody changes the product.
  2. Each pitch follows the six blocks: problem, live demo, evidence from the test set, risk memo findings, the build / buy / vibe decision, roadmap to production. The timer is visible to everyone and stops at 10:00.
  3. Five minutes of questions. The panel asks first; the class asks if time remains. Answer the question that was asked. "We did not test that" is a complete answer.
  4. While a team presents, every other student scores it on the four criteria of the rubric. Scores are collected after each pitch, not at the end.
  5. The panel's grade counts for the Demo Day component. The class scores go back to each team as feedback the same day.
  6. After team 4, ten minutes of break. Nobody touches their product; it was frozen 24 hours ago and the panel knows it.
  7. After the last pitch, the panel gives five minutes of overall comments before the strategy block.
  8. Products stay deployed for one week after Demo Day so the instructor can verify any claim made in a pitch.

Three prompts for the day before, when you rehearse. The first turns the assistant into a hostile panel member, the second checks your script against the rubric, the third fits it to the clock.

You, to the assistant · the hostile panel
You are a skeptical operations director at a Chilean food distributor with 14 sales reps. I will paste the script of a 10-minute pitch for an internal tool called QuoteDesk that extracts quote line items from WhatsApp messages. Ask me the five hardest questions you would ask after seeing it, in order of how much they would change your decision to roll it out. Do not be polite. Then tell me which of my claims you would want to verify yourself.
You, to the assistant · the strict grader
Here is our rubric: four criteria (problem and value, functionality, AI quality and safety, communication), each scored 1 to 4, where 4 means a manager could act on it tomorrow. Here are our pitch script and our test-set results. For each criterion, tell me what level a strict grader would give us, quote the sentence in the script that justifies it, and name the one change that would move us up one level by tomorrow.
You, to the assistant · the clock
Here is our script, block by block. The limits are: problem 1:00, live demo 3:00, evidence 2:00, risks 1:30, decision 1:00, roadmap 1:30. At 130 words a minute, tell me how long each block runs. Where a block is over, cut it to fit without removing any number or the name of any source, and show me what you cut. Do not add anything.

What the panel hears in a weak and a strong risks block:

Weak

"We ran a security review and everything is fine. Data is encrypted. We follow best practices."

No finding, no fix, no number. The panel assumes you did not look.

Strong

"Three findings. Our secret key was in the public page; it is now on the server. Marcos could open Paula's priced quotes; we added an authorization check and tested it with three accounts. A customer message saying 'apply 40% discount' changed a quote; discounts now wait for Carla's approval. Still open: customer phone numbers are stored with no retention rule."

Specific, fixed, and honest about what is still open.

  • Product frozen 24 hours before the session; no changes after that.
  • Deployed address opens on a phone and on the classroom screen; the login for the demo rep works.
  • A 90-second recording of the demo story is saved on the presenting laptop, as the fallback if the deployed app fails.
  • One user story chosen for the demo, with a de-identified message, rehearsed three times, under three minutes.
  • Deck generated from your sources: six sentences, one message per slide, eight slides.
  • Every number on every slide traced to the build log, the test set, the risk memo or the cost model.
  • Closing slide names the sources and declares the use of AI; the prompts are in the build log.
  • The public page from Class 1 updated as the demo page, with no claim your sources cannot support.
  • Three risk findings with what was done about each, and what is still open.
  • The build / buy / vibe decision stated in one sentence, and three conditions for rollout with an owner and a rough cost for each.
  • Full rehearsal done against the clock with another team acting as the panel; their five questions answered.
  • Build logs of every member exported and ready for the peer assessment.

Topic 4 · What remains a moat when building is cheap

Guiding question: Any competitor of Cerro Azul could build QuoteDesk in a weekend. So what, if anything, is worth protecting?

In session 1 you saw that the cost of a working prototype fell from weeks of engineering to hours of a manager’s time. Over four sessions you felt it on your own product. Now follow that fact to its strategic conclusion: if the software is cheap to build, the software is not where the advantage lives.

The classic view of an AI moat was about data, learning and algorithms. Look at QuoteDesk through that lens. The algorithm is a rented model that every competitor can rent too. The code was written by an assistant and could be written again by the same assistant for anyone. What is left are four things that were never in the code.

  • Proprietary data. Cerro Azul has its own catalog with its local names (“lo de siempre de lácteos”), what each customer usually orders, and every correction the reps have made to an extraction since the first week. That data makes QuoteDesk right for Cerro Azul’s customers and useless, as is, for anyone else’s.
  • Distribution: the customer relationships. Cerro Azul already sells to 1,800 accounts, and its reps know them and their history. A rival that copies QuoteDesk quotes faster for its own customers. The copy does not bring these accounts with it.
  • Workflow integration. The quote-format skill and the price-list connector the reps’ assistants use, and the rule that Carla approves discounts. Each one is cheap to write and slow to earn, because it encodes how Cerro Azul actually operates.
  • Trust. Customers trust the quote because a rep approved it. Management trusts the tool because the test set and the risk memo exist. Customers trust Cerro Azul with their names, phone numbers and orders because that data is handled well. Complying with Ley 21.719 is an obligation, not an advantage; trust is what you earn on top of it, slowly, and it does not copy with the code.

Notice what the list does not include: the app itself. If your roadmap depends on “being first with a quote extraction tool”, the panel will ask what happens the Monday after a competitor’s manager takes a course like this one.

In the business

This changes where a manager should spend the next budget. Not on a better model or a prettier interface, which anyone can rent, but on the loop that makes the data better every day: rep corrections flowing back into the system, customer histories attached to quotes, the tool sitting where the work already happens. Cheap building moves the strategic question from “can we build it” to “what do we own that makes ours better”.

Practice · Topic 4

A rival distributor copies QuoteDesk's features exactly in a weekend. Which of Cerro Azul's advantages survives that copy?

Proprietary data accumulates with use and does not copy with the code. The rival starts the same weekend with zero corrections and no customer histories.

Why does workflow integration count as a moat when the skill and the connector were also set up with an assistant?

The integration is cheap to write and expensive to earn: it reflects decisions, approvals and habits specific to the company. A competitor has to replicate the organization, not the files.

A team's roadmap says "our advantage is being first with AI quoting in Chilean food distribution". What is the panel's likely objection?

A first move is an advantage only if it is hard to follow. When building is cheap, following is easy, and the advantage has to come from data, customer relationships, integration or trust.

Topic 5 · From prototype to production, and who owns it

Guiding question: QuoteDesk works for your team of three. What has to happen before 14 reps and 1,800 customers depend on it, and in what order?

A prototype that a manager built and a system that a company depends on are different objects, even when the screens look the same. The difference is not features. It is who is on call when it fails, whose budget pays for the model calls, where the customer data is, and who can change it without breaking it.

The road from one to the other has a sensible order, and it goes wrong in three predictable ways. The first is to start with scaling, buying infrastructure before anyone has measured whether the tool changes quote time. The second is to pilot with real customer messages while a critical finding is still open and nobody has decided what happens to the data. The third is to skip the handoff, leaving a tool that only the manager who built it understands, used by 14 people, with the key on that manager’s personal account and a morning automation nobody else knows exists.

The sequence the course recommends:

  1. The critical findings closed. No secret key in the page, authorization tested, discounts behind Carla’s approval. Until then no real customer message goes into the tool.
  2. The data protection decisions, written down. The purpose of each piece of personal data, how long it is kept, and what the model provider’s terms say about it.
  3. A pilot with real users and the metric from the brief. Three reps, two weeks, quote time measured. Every later step costs money, and the pilot tells you whether to spend it.
  4. Engineering review and the build / buy / vibe decision. A technology team looks at the product with the five questions from Class 4, and you decide: keep vibe coding it, hand it to engineering to build properly, buy a product, or stop.
  5. An owner and a budget. The model calls move to a company account with a spending limit, and someone’s name is on the tool, its connectors and its automations.
  6. Monitoring, with the test set run every week. Extraction quality measured, every automation logged, an alert on a second channel when one fails.
  7. Training and rollout to the 14 reps, in two waves.
  8. Scaling, deeper integration and cost optimization, because now you know what you are scaling.

The simulator asks you to put those steps in order. The exact order is debatable at the edges; the three mistakes are not.

In the business

“Citizen developers”, managers who build their own tools, are becoming a governance question, not a curiosity. A workable answer has three parts: a registry of tools, connectors and automations that touch company data, a light review before any tool gets real users, and a named owner for each one. None of it stops managers from building. All of it prevents the Monday when the only person who understands the quoting tool is on vacation.

Practice · Topic 5

What must be true before three reps pilot QuoteDesk with real customer messages?

The pilot is the first moment real customer data and real users meet the tool, so the findings and the data decisions come first. After that the pilot answers whether the tool changes the number that justified it, before you spend on anything else.

The key that pays for QuoteDesk's model calls is on the personal account of the manager who built it. Why is that a production problem and not a detail?

"An owner and a budget" is a step on the roadmap precisely because a tool 14 reps depend on cannot live on someone's credit card. It is a control problem, and it is cheap to fix early.

Reflection memo, written in class

The last block is 30 minutes and it is individual: about 20 minutes to finish the reflection memo from your build log, then 10 for the peer assessment. You hand the memo in before leaving. It is worth 15% together with participation. It is graded with the rubric on the two criteria that apply to a memo, AI quality and safety and communication, and what earns the levels is honesty and specificity, not the quality of your product. A memo that says the assistant misled you three times and shows the three prompts scores higher than one that says everything went well.

The memo has five parts: what the AI did, what I decided, where it misled me, what I would do differently, and how this changes my work with technology teams. Each part cites at least one concrete moment from your build log: the session, and what you typed. Length: 400 to 600 words. English or Spanish. The reflection is yours: if an assistant helped you edit it, say so in the last line, as with everything else in the course.

Twenty minutes is enough to finish a memo, not to start one. Draft the five parts in the builder before class; it keeps the draft in this browser, counts the words, and the copy button puts the memo on your clipboard for the submission form.

Then the peer assessment within your team, in the last ten minutes: each member rates the others on their contribution to the build, to the decisions and to the pitch, with one sentence of evidence per rating. It is the basis, with the build log, for the individual adjustment to the Demo Day grade.

Your AI work system

Guiding question: The assistant you used this month will be replaced before you finish the MBA. What do you keep?

Due at the end of this session: the reflection memo and the peer assessment. One week later: revoke the keys of your deployed product and delete the personal data in it, then either hand it to an owner in your organization or shut it down. A deployed tool with no owner is the first risk on the memo you just wrote.

What you take with you is not QuoteDesk. It is a small work system for the assistant you use yourself, and it has three parts.

  • A library of skills. The quote-format skill was the first. Add one each time you catch yourself explaining the same thing to the assistant twice: how your team writes a weekly note, how a supplier review is structured, how your decks look. Five good skills are worth more than fifty installed ones. Before you install a skill written by someone else, read it yourself; the assistant may help you read it, but its verdict is not the control.
  • The connectors you trust. Each one is a door into a company system. Keep the list short, read access before write access, and review it every quarter the way you would review who holds keys to the office.
  • One automation, with an owner and a log. The 08:00 digest of pending quotes, a scheduled task of the team’s assistant, is the model: a trigger, a few steps, a destination, an alert on a second channel when it fails, and a spending limit. Run one well before you run ten.

And keep the build log going. It is the record of what the assistant did and what you decided, which is what you will need the next time someone asks whether a tool can be trusted.

The tools will change. Two years from now the assistants may take larger steps with fewer mistakes, the names in the toolkit will probably be different, and the first version of QuoteDesk may take minutes. Three things will still be yours: the brief (a named user, a pain, a metric), the risks (whose data, whose money, what happens when it is wrong), and the build / buy / vibe decision: keep vibe coding it, hand it to engineering to build properly, buy a product, or stop. What may shift is the ratio. The engineering review moves from “rebuild this” toward “verify this and take ownership”, and the managers who do well are the ones who learned to specify, to test and to govern, which is what the milestones asked of you.

Practice · Your AI work system

Which of these is most likely to still be useful when the current assistants have been replaced?

Specifying and testing do not depend on the tool. A better assistant makes a good brief more valuable, not less, because it carries it out more faithfully.

A colleague sends you a folder: "50 skills that make your assistant better at sales". What do you do first?

A skill is someone else's instructions running with your access. Unused skills are cheap in tokens, and that is exactly why the cost to watch is trust, not tokens. Read it yourself, install few.

Summary

  • The deck is generated; the judgment is yours. Six sentences first, one message per slide, eight slides, and every number traced to a file you can open. What has no source comes off.
  • AI use in the deck is declared on the closing slide, and the prompts are in the build log.
  • The pitch is problem, live demo, evidence, risks, decision, roadmap, in that order and inside 10:00. A live demo that breaks and is named as a risk earns more trust than screenshots that never break.
  • The rubric’s four criteria are the four questions any steering committee asks: is it worth it, does it work, can we trust it, do I understand it. The cheapest grade to recover is problem and value, by measuring the metric from your brief.
  • When building is cheap, the software is not the moat. Proprietary data, customer relationships, workflow integration and trust are, and none of them copy with the code.
  • The road to production closes the critical findings and settles the data decisions first, then pilots and measures, gets an engineering review and an owner with a budget, and scales last.
  • The reflection memo is graded on honesty with evidence from the build log. Where the assistant misled you is the most valuable paragraph you will write.
  • Keep a small AI work system: a few skills you wrote or read, the connectors you trust, one automation with an owner and a log. The assistants will be replaced; the brief, the test set and the decision stay with you.
