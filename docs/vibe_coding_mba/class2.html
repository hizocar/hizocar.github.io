Topic 1 · Prompting as specification

Guiding question: if the assistant builds exactly what you asked, why does it so often build the wrong thing?

In session 1 your team wrote a brief and got a working page out of it in under an hour. That felt like magic. The second hour usually feels different: the assistant adds a button you never asked for, the list sorts the wrong way, the “save” works on your laptop and not on your phone. None of that is the assistant being dumb. It is the assistant filling the gaps you left, with the most common guess from millions of other apps. A quote tool for a distributor in Chile is not the most common app.

The fix is a habit, not a tool. Before you ask for a feature, write it as a user story with acceptance criteria. A user story is one sentence: as a sales rep, I want to paste a customer message and save it as a quote request, so that I stop retyping it into the ERP. Acceptance criteria are the three to six checks that tell you, and the assistant, when the story is done: the customer is chosen from the existing customer list, not typed; the saved request shows date, rep and customer; an empty message cannot be saved; the list shows the newest request first.

Managers already know this format from product teams. What is new is that the assistant reads it literally. Each criterion you write is one decision the assistant does not have to guess. Each one you leave out is a surprise waiting for you in the next hour.

In the business

A rep who retypes quotes is slow. A manager who specifies badly is expensive: every vague request costs a build, a test, a complaint and a rebuild. The assistant is cheap per attempt, but your team’s two hours in this lab are not. Treat acceptance criteria as the cheapest insurance you will ever buy.

Practice · Topic 1

Which of these is an acceptance criterion, as opposed to a wish?

A criterion is a check that is either true or false when you use the app. "Cannot be saved when empty" is one. "Fast and easy" is a wish, and the assistant will decide for you what it means.

Your assistant added a "priority" dropdown that nobody asked for. What most likely happened?

Every unspecified field is a guess. Listing the fields of a quote request (customer, message, date, rep, status) in the criteria removes the guess, and the dropdown.

How many acceptance criteria should a user story carry for the assistant?

Three to six criteria is the sweet spot: fields, the rules that matter, and what happens when input is bad. More than that is a sign the story should be split.

Topic 2 · Small steps beat big bangs

Guiding question: why does asking for ten features at once take longer than asking for them one at a time?

Here is the pattern every first-time builder repeats. The brief is ready, confidence is high, and the team pastes a single request: login, customer list, quote request form, item table, PDF export, email notification, dashboard. The assistant works for a few minutes and produces something that mostly runs. Then the login breaks the list, the PDF breaks the form, and nobody knows which of the seven changes caused which of the four problems.

The alternative is boring and it works: one story per request. Ask for the customer picker. Open the app and use it as a rep would. If it works, save a checkpoint (topic 3). If it does not, you know exactly what broke, because only one thing changed. Then ask for the next story.

The rule has a second half: test by using, not by reading. You are not going to read the code, and you do not need to. Use the app the way a rep would, on a phone if that is where reps will use it. When something looks wrong, ask the assistant: what did you change and why? The explanation is written for you, in plain language, and it is often where you discover the gap in your criteria.

In the business

Small steps are how good operations teams already work: change one thing on the line, measure, keep or revert. The assistant makes the cycle minutes instead of weeks, which means the discipline matters more, not less. A team that batches twenty changes has thrown away the main advantage of the tool.

Practice · Topic 2

After a request for three features, the quote list stopped loading. What is the fastest way to find the cause?

Going back to the last known-good state and re-adding changes one by one isolates the cause in minutes. This is why checkpoints and small steps belong together.

What does "test by using" mean for a manager who cannot read code?

The test is the task: paste a real message, pick a real customer, save, refresh, try an empty message, try it on a phone. If the rep could not do it, it is not done.

Topic 3 · Checkpoints, branches and undo

Guiding question: how do you make it safe to break the app?

Software teams use a tool called version control, and the most common one is Git, hosted on GitHub. You will use it through the assistant, so you need three ideas, not the commands.

A commit is a checkpoint: a named snapshot of the whole app at a moment you were happy with it. “Customer picker working” is a good name. You make one after every story that passes your test. A branch is an experiment: a copy of the app where you can try something risky, like redesigning the quote list, without touching the version that works. If the experiment is good, you merge it back. If it is bad, you delete the branch and nothing happened. Rollback means returning the app to any earlier checkpoint. It takes seconds, and it is what makes small steps safe: the worst case of any request is “go back twenty minutes”.

The assistant does all of this when you ask in plain language: save a checkpoint called “login working”, start a branch to try a new layout for the list, go back to the checkpoint before the PDF export. GitHub keeps the history online, so your teammates and the instructor see the same timeline, and milestone M2 is the link to it.

In the business

The repository is also the audit trail. When a rep asks why the discount field disappeared last Tuesday, the timeline answers in one click. When you hand the prototype to an engineering team in session 4, the history of what was tried and why is worth more to them than the code itself.

Practice · Topic 3

When should you save a checkpoint?

A checkpoint is a known-good state. Save it when the test passes and name it after what works, so that rolling back is a choice between meaningful states, not timestamps.

Your teammate wants to try a radical redesign of the quote list while you keep adding the login. What should they do?

A branch is exactly for this: an experiment that cannot break the working app. Merge it when it passes the test, or discard it at no cost.

Topic 4 · Where the data lives

Guiding question: why did the quote requests disappear when a rep refreshed the page?

The first version of QuoteDesk kept quote requests “in the page”: they existed only in the browser that created them. Refresh, and they are gone. Open the app on a second phone, and the list is empty. This is normal for a first hour and useless for a second rep.

There are three places data can live. In the page: fast, free, vanishes on refresh. In a file on the server: survives refresh, but two reps saving at the same time can overwrite each other, and there is no way to say who may see what. In a managed database: a service, such as the one in the course toolkit, that stores rows, serves many users at once, and comes with authentication (who are you: the login) built in. For anything two people will use, the database is the answer, and the assistant can set it up for you in one request.

Authentication is the other half of “real users”. A login means each quote request is tied to the rep who created it, which is what you need for the list “my requests”, for the manager’s view, and for the authorization rules you will add in session 4. The assistant will build the login screen, the password reset and the session handling; your job is to specify who logs in and what they should see first.

In the business

“Where does the data live” is the question legal, IT and finance will ask you first, long before they ask what the app does. Knowing that quote requests sit in a managed database, in a named region, with a login per rep, is the difference between a prototype that can be piloted with real customers and one that cannot leave your laptop.

Practice · Topic 4

Two reps use QuoteDesk at the same time. Which storage choice handles this safely?

A managed database is built for many users writing at once and ties every row to the user who created it. The other two options break as soon as there is a second person.

What does authentication give you, in one line?

Authentication answers "who are you". Authorization answers "what may you do". QuoteDesk v1 needs the first; session 4 adds the second.

Topic 5 · UX for output that is sometimes wrong

Guiding question: a rep will trust QuoteDesk once. What makes them trust it the second time?

Next session QuoteDesk will read a pasted message and propose the items: “20 boxes of 900 cc oil, 5 sacks of rice, the usual dairy”. The last one is a guess. If the app shows that guess with the same confidence as the first two, the rep learns, after one wrong delivery, that nothing on the screen can be trusted. Then they check every line, and the app saves nobody any time.

Four patterns fix this, and they are decisions you make in the spec, not in the code. Show confidence: lines the model is unsure about look different and say so. Stream the result: items appear as they are produced, so a three-second wait feels like progress instead of a frozen screen. Honest errors: when the model cannot read the message, the app says “I could not find items in this message” and offers the manual form, instead of an empty table or a generic error. Let the user correct: every proposed line can be edited in place, and the correction is saved, so the app is never the last word.

These patterns are what separate an AI demo from an AI product. The model will be wrong some of the time no matter what you do. The experience decides whether being wrong costs a glance or costs a customer.

In the business

Trust is a metric you can measure: how often reps edit the proposed lines, how often they abandon the tool and go back to retyping, how many wrong deliveries trace back to a quote. Put the correction button in and you also get the data to improve the model later. Leave it out and you get complaints.

Practice · Topic 5

The model is unsure whether "lo de siempre de lácteos" means the customer's usual dairy order. What should the screen do?

Visible uncertainty plus an easy correction is the pattern. The rep spends two seconds on the one line that needs it and trusts the rest.

Why stream results instead of showing them all at once after three seconds?

Perceived latency is what matters to the user. Three seconds of visible progress feel shorter than three seconds of a spinner, and the rep gets a head start on checking.

The model cannot find any items in a message that is just "hola, me llamas?". What is the honest error?

An honest error says what happened and what the user can do next. It keeps the rep inside the tool instead of sending them back to the old way.

Build lab · QuoteDesk v1: login, real data, rollback

What your team builds in the next 120 minutes

Teams of 2 to 3 · the instructor circulates · save a checkpoint every 20 minutes

  1. Move the project to the agentic assistant and GitHub. Open your QuoteDesk v0 from session 1 in the agentic coding assistant from the toolkit. Ask it to create a GitHub repository for the project and save a first checkpoint called "v0 from session 1". Open the repository in the browser and confirm the checkpoint is there.
  2. Write the first story with criteria. In your shared document, write the user story for saving a quote request with 3 to 6 acceptance criteria (fields, rules, failure case). Paste it to the assistant as one request. Do not add other features in the same message.
  3. Test by using. Open the app, paste a real customer message from your team's case, pick a customer, save, refresh. Try an empty message. Try it on a phone. If anything fails, ask the assistant "what did you change and why?" and adjust the criteria before asking for a fix.
  4. Save a checkpoint named after what works, for example "quote request saves with customer and date".
  5. Add the managed database. Ask the assistant to store quote requests in the managed database from the toolkit, keeping the same fields. Test: save a request on one device, open the app on another, the request is there. Checkpoint.
  6. Add the login. Ask for a login for reps with email and password, password reset, and the rule that each request is tied to the rep who created it and the list shows "my requests" first. Test with two different rep accounts. Checkpoint.
  7. Break something on purpose and roll back. Ask for a risky change you do not need, such as "replace the list with a calendar view". Confirm it broke or changed something you did not want. Ask the assistant to roll back to the last checkpoint. Confirm the app is exactly as before. This step is graded.
  8. Start a branch for an experiment. One teammate asks for a new layout of the quote list on a branch while another keeps testing the main version. Merge only if the test passes.
  9. Apply two UX patterns. Add the honest empty state ("no requests yet, paste a message to start") and a loading state when the list is fetching. Mark in your build log which patterns from topic 5 you chose and why.
  10. Deploy and share. Publish the app to its public URL, open it on a phone, and paste the URL, the repository link and the checkpoint list into your team's milestone M2 document.

Critique and reflection

Two teams show one user story end to end: paste a message, pick the customer, save, refresh, log in as a second rep. The class applies the rubric with the criterion in focus for this session, functionality: does the product work for its user end to end, including the failure cases? The class also asks the presenting team to roll back one change live.

Reflection prompt for your build log: what did the assistant guess that you should have specified, and what would the criterion have said?

Summary

  • The assistant builds what you specify and guesses the rest. Acceptance criteria remove the guesses.
  • One story per request. Test by using, not by reading. Ask “what did you change and why?”
  • A checkpoint is a known-good state with a name. Save one after every story that passes.
  • A branch is an experiment that cannot break the working app. Rollback is the safety net that makes small steps cheap.
  • Data for two or more people belongs in a managed database with a login per user.
  • Authentication is “who are you”. Authorization, “what may you do”, comes in session 4.
  • Four UX patterns for output that is sometimes wrong: visible confidence, streaming, honest errors, user correction.
  • Milestone M2 is the proof: deployed, with login, with data, with three stories and a history you can roll back.
